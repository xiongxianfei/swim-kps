# Progress a controlled swimming task

## Key takeaway

Extend a manageable task only after its breathing, stopping and coordination are reliable.

## Summary

Distance adds demand. Combining longer attempts, harder effort and less rest makes it difficult to identify what exceeded control. Select a modest change only from a task already manageable with reliable stopping and breathing. Longer distance, more effort and less rest should not all change in the same comparison.

## Inputs and prerequisites

Self-coaching does not mean swimming alone. Use a suitable lifeguarded pool with a buddy and the close assistance needed; weak or inexperienced swimmers need an appropriately capable helper within reach. Attempt only tasks in which breathing, stopping and regaining support are already dependable; otherwise use land study or in-person instruction first. Do not hyperventilate, restrict breaths, prolong submersion or force painful joint positions. Stop for pain, dizziness, distress or unreliable air/support; get immediate assistance for serious symptoms.

## Local explanatory basis

The effects of component movements depend partly on their timing relative to other movements and the breathing opportunity. A sustainable breathing pattern depends on coordinating ventilation, a clear route to air and the ongoing stroke task.

## Objective
Extend a manageable task only after its breathing, stopping and coordination are reliable.

## Rationale
Distance adds demand. Combining longer attempts, harder effort and less rest makes it difficult to identify what exceeded control.

## Evidence and reasoning
[Swimming Safety](../references/american-red-cross-undated-swimming-safety.md#supervision-companions-and-close-assistance) sets the safety context. The one-demand-at-a-time progression is an authored conservative choice, not a research-proven distance schedule.

## Steps
1. Begin from a task you can finish with normal air access and a deliberate safe stop.
2. Decide which one demand changes: a modest extension, another controlled repeat or a familiar wall transition. Keep other demands comfortable.
3. Rest normally; do not make reduced breathing or reduced rest the route to a longer swim.
4. Stop when form, orientation or breathing ceases to be manageable. Use shorter attempts rather than a survival finish.
5. Check whether the intended technique remains available later in the task and on another occasion.

## Expected effect
A bounded increase in usable practice, not a compulsory metre milestone.

## Validation and counterevidence
If coordination or safe recovery is lost, return to the previous manageable demand. A completed distance does not prove technique efficiency.

## Limits
No prescribed 50-to-100-to-200 m ladder, race turns, underwater pullouts or open-water transfer. No current user distance is assumed.
