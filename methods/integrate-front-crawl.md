# Check front crawl integration

## Key takeaway

Find whether a component adjustment survives alternating arms, kick and breathing.

## Summary

Whole-stroke demands differ from isolated practice. The relevant question is whether the adjustment helps the actual task without creating another limiting problem. After checking the arm or breathing component, return to a short familiar crawl. If the cue disrupts alternating arms or creates frantic kicking, reduce the demand and investigate one interaction.

## Inputs and prerequisites

Self-coaching does not mean swimming alone. Use a suitable lifeguarded pool with a buddy and the close assistance needed; weak or inexperienced swimmers need an appropriately capable helper within reach. Attempt only tasks in which breathing, stopping and regaining support are already dependable; otherwise use land study or in-person instruction first. Do not hyperventilate, restrict breaths, prolong submersion or force painful joint positions. Stop for pain, dizziness, distress or unreliable air/support; get immediate assistance for serious symptoms.

## Local explanatory basis

Performance under a simplified drill’s conditions does not by itself establish performance under whole-stroke conditions. The effects of component movements depend partly on their timing relative to other movements and the breathing opportunity.

## Objective
Find whether a component adjustment survives alternating arms, kick and breathing.

## Rationale
Whole-stroke demands differ from isolated practice. The relevant question is whether the adjustment helps the actual task without creating another limiting problem.

## Evidence and reasoning
[Front-crawl cycle model](../models/front-crawl-cycle-model.md) represents the interactions; [Isolated success does not establish transfer](../principles/isolated-success-does-not-establish-transfer.md) is a task-comparison argument, not a proof of a special drill sequence.

## Steps
1. Set one whole-stroke target, such as an entry that remains on its own track during a breath. Choose a safe familiar short attempt.
2. Observe that event during normal alternating arms, manageable kick and ordinary breathing. Separate visible execution from sensation and interpretation.
3. Use a brief easier rehearsal only when it answers a specific problem. Do not add a full curriculum of drills or remove essential support.
4. Return to the same whole-stroke task with one cue. Avoid imposing a lead-arm hold or catch-up pause merely because a drill used it.
5. Check whether the movement changed and whether breath, direction and comfort were retained. Lower task demand when several components fail together.
6. Retest a promising change later without constant cueing. Record a provisional choice, unresolved finding or need for external observation.

## Expected effect
A decision about whether the component is useful in front crawl, not merely executable in a drill.

## Validation and counterevidence
If the benefit disappears in the whole stroke or requires unmanageable attention, revise the cue, demand or hypothesis.

## Limits
No compulsory component-learning order or claim that successful integration on one day establishes durable learning.
