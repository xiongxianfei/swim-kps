# Integrate front crawl side breathing

## Key takeaway

Connect access to air with the arm cycle and body turn rather than enforce a particular stroke count.

## Summary

A breath changes head/body orientation during another movement. If the air window is too short or demand too high, a head cue alone may not resolve the problem. Observe when the turn begins relative to the breathing-side arm. Aim for clear air access with a smooth return, not a forced one-goggle picture or a compulsory every-third-stroke pattern.

## Inputs and prerequisites

Self-coaching does not mean swimming alone. Use a suitable lifeguarded pool with a buddy and the close assistance needed; weak or inexperienced swimmers need an appropriately capable helper within reach. Attempt only tasks in which breathing, stopping and regaining support are already dependable; otherwise use land study or in-person instruction first. Do not hyperventilate, restrict breaths, prolong submersion or force painful joint positions. Stop for pain, dizziness, distress or unreliable air/support; get immediate assistance for serious symptoms.

## Local explanatory basis

A sustainable breathing pattern depends on coordinating ventilation, a clear route to air and the ongoing stroke task. A sensation, visible motion or single performance change can be consistent with more than one explanation.

## Objective
Connect access to air with the arm cycle and body turn rather than enforce a particular stroke count.

## Rationale
A breath changes head/body orientation during another movement. If the air window is too short or demand too high, a head cue alone may not resolve the problem.

## Evidence and reasoning
[Improve your breathing in front crawl](../references/swim-england-2018-front-crawl-breathing.md#exhalation-options-and-breath-timing) and [Freestyle Breathing: The Complete Guide](../references/us-masters-swimming-undated-freestyle-breathing.md#air-access-with-rotation-and-recovery) support adaptable patterns and integrated turning. These are coaching accounts; the exercise sequence is authored.

## Steps
1. Confirm a comfortable supported breath and known recovery. If an unsupported turn is not safe, use qualified in-person support and land rehearsal first.
2. Choose the easier side and an ordinary breathing frequency that supplies air. Do not impose bilateral or every-third-stroke breathing.
3. In a familiar easy crawl, turn with body roll as the breathing-side arm progresses back toward recovery. Do not wait until the hand is about to re-enter before searching for air.
4. Inhale when the mouth is clear; do not depend on a bow-wave trough or one-goggle position. Let the opposite arm continue its coordinated role, not a forced downward support press.
5. Return the face smoothly as the recovery arm comes forward, using face-back-before-hand-entry as a starting checkpoint. Air access takes priority over rushing to meet that checkpoint.
6. Compare one timing adjustment with a safe usual/reference version. Evaluate breathing plus continuing arm/kick rhythm; do not use restricted-breathing drills.
7. If clearance improves but unusual breathlessness persists, stop and seek appropriate help rather than attributing everything to stroke timing.

## Expected effect
A less hurried, reproducible breathing opportunity within the actual stroke.

## Validation and counterevidence
If clearance improves but breathlessness remains, consider demand or seek appropriate help; do not assume the neck angle caused everything.

## Limits
No mandatory every-third-stroke pattern, bilateral breathing requirement, restricted-breathing drill or deliberate hyperventilation.

## Selected reference and limits of its basis
The return-before-hand-entry checkpoint is a source-informed reference, not a universal requirement at every pace. The USMS breathing page also includes racing breath-reduction tasks and forced-expiration imagery; those are not adopted. The current Method uses comfortable exhalation and sufficient air, and excludes any drill that relies on tolerating air hunger.
