# Check breaststroke coordination in the whole stroke

## Key takeaway

Find where arm, breath, recovery and kick events stop working together.

## Summary

Isolated actions change meaning when their timing overlaps. A whole-stroke check tests the task you actually want to improve. Observe the main kick relative to the returning arms. A useful starting aim is for the arms to be reaching forward as the kick acts and then to regain length. The observation matters more than counting four verbal phases.

## Inputs and prerequisites

Self-coaching does not mean swimming alone. Use a suitable lifeguarded pool with a buddy and the close assistance needed; weak or inexperienced swimmers need an appropriately capable helper within reach. Attempt only tasks in which breathing, stopping and regaining support are already dependable; otherwise use land study or in-person instruction first. Do not hyperventilate, restrict breaths, prolong submersion or force painful joint positions. Stop for pain, dizziness, distress or unreliable air/support; get immediate assistance for serious symptoms.

## Local explanatory basis

The effects of component movements depend partly on their timing relative to other movements and the breathing opportunity. Performance under a simplified drill’s conditions does not by itself establish performance under whole-stroke conditions.

## Objective
Find where arm, breath, recovery and kick events stop working together.

## Rationale
Isolated actions change meaning when their timing overlaps. A whole-stroke check tests the task you actually want to improve.

## Evidence and reasoning
[Tips for getting breaststroke right](../references/swim-england-2016-tips-for-getting-breaststroke-right.md#basic-breaststroke-sequence) supplies a beginner rhythm cue. [Comparison of the arm-stroke kinematics between maximal and sub-maximal breaststroke swimming using discrete data and time series analysis](../references/gourgoulis-2022-breaststroke-arm-kinematics.md#pace-related-phase-differences) limits the notion of a universal timing pattern.

## Steps
1. Choose a safe short ordinary breaststroke segment with a known finish. Name one event relationship to inspect.
2. Use outward/inward arms with the breath opportunity; let forward arm recovery and leg preparation connect rather than impose perfect stillness of the legs.
3. As the arms approach a comfortable forward position, complete the prepared kick and return the legs toward length. This is an easy starting reference, not a fixed phase law.
4. Recognize completion and begin the next cycle before waiting compromises air or control. Do not turn pull/breathe/kick/glide into four holds.
5. Find the earliest repeatable disruption. If needed, rehearse that event with support, then rejoin the whole stroke at a lower demand.
6. Check execution, breathing, comfort and safe finishing together; later retest without the cue before treating it as reliable.

## Expected effect
A clearer event relationship with retained breathing and control.

## Validation and counterevidence
If the cue improves one event but worsens the breath or kick, it has not solved the integrated task. No visible timing change leaves the hypothesis untested.

## Limits
No stopwatch-defined phase duration or copying maximal-race timing into beginner swimming.
