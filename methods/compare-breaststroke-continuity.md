# Compare breaststroke continuity

## Key takeaway

Explore whether the next action is rushed or delayed for the current task without prescribing glide seconds.

## Summary

A longer pause can lower stroke frequency while allowing more deceleration; a shorter pause can interrupt completion. Neither direction is automatically better. Instead of imposing a two-second glide, compare the next pull after completed extension with my safe usual rhythm. Stop the comparison if waiting creates loss of air, control or a stalled task.

## Inputs and prerequisites

Self-coaching does not mean swimming alone. Use a suitable lifeguarded pool with a buddy and the close assistance needed; weak or inexperienced swimmers need an appropriately capable helper within reach. Attempt only tasks in which breathing, stopping and regaining support are already dependable; otherwise use land study or in-person instruction first. Do not hyperventilate, restrict breaths, prolong submersion or force painful joint positions. Stop for pain, dizziness, distress or unreliable air/support; get immediate assistance for serious symptoms.

## Local explanatory basis

The effects of component movements depend partly on their timing relative to other movements and the breathing opportunity. Stroke count alone does not determine speed, metabolic cost or whole-stroke effectiveness.

## Objective
Explore whether the next action is rushed or delayed for the current task without prescribing glide seconds.

## Rationale
A longer pause can lower stroke frequency while allowing more deceleration; a shorter pause can interrupt completion. Neither direction is automatically better.

## Evidence and reasoning
[Comparison of the arm-stroke kinematics between maximal and sub-maximal breaststroke swimming using discrete data and time series analysis](../references/gourgoulis-2022-breaststroke-arm-kinematics.md#pace-related-phase-differences) reports pace-sensitive phases in trained swimmers. [Comparable-swim measurement model](../models/stroke-measurement-model.md) explains why count alone is insufficient. The comparison is a personal inference, not a validated novice timing formula.

## Steps
1. Use only an already controlled short breaststroke task. Keep ordinary breathing and rest.
2. Observe completion and the start of the next arm action. Record rushed recovery or loss of control before changing timing.
3. Compare one small comfortable timing adjustment with a safe usual/reference pattern. Do not deliberately wait until air or support becomes uncertain.
4. Consider task time, count convention, effort, breathing and execution together.
5. Keep a change only provisionally and retest without forced counting on another occasion.

## Expected effect
A personally manageable continuity choice with an explicit context.

## Validation and counterevidence
Lower count accompanied by slower time, higher effort or worse air access does not demonstrate better efficiency. No repeated distinction remains unresolved.

## Limits
No fixed 1-, 2- or 3-second glide, mandatory pause, underwater-distance test or maximum-speed protocol.
