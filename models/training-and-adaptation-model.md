# Training and adaptation model

## Key takeaway

Repeated workload, recovery and the swimmer's changing state jointly influence later capacity; a single performance cannot isolate adaptation.

## Summary

External workload describes activity; internal load describes its response in the person. Training can stimulate lasting change while also producing temporary fatigue. Recovery and prior state influence what follows. Observable performance mixes capacity, skill, current fatigue and task conditions, so improvement is not a direct measurement of one physiological mechanism.

## Purpose and boundary

Explain the difference between completing more work, recovering from an attempt and developing capacity over time. This is an introductory qualitative system for surface-swimming study, not a training schedule, injury predictor or fitted fitness-fatigue equation.

## Essential terms and components

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| External workload | Activity described by duration, distance, frequency, intensity and task conditions |
| Internal load | Physiological and psychological response to that activity |
| Fatigue | A temporary reduction in ability to sustain the relevant performance; mechanisms and duration can differ |
| Recovery | Processes and conditions through which the consequences of a demand resolve |
| Adaptation | A relatively persistent change in capacity associated with repeated exposure |
| Specificity | Changes relate to the functions and demands repeatedly engaged, rather than guaranteeing improvement in every task |

## Representation and mechanism

```text
workload + prior state + wider conditions
                     ↓
               individual response
                 ↙             ↘
       temporary fatigue     adaptive stimulus
                 ↘             ↙
                recovery over time
                         ↓
       later state → later response to a specified task
```

The branches can coexist. This is not a guaranteed upward progression: more work does not ensure a greater useful change, and recovery is not a fixed clock. Repeated endurance activity can increase muscle mitochondrial capacity and capillary supply, supporting aerobic ATP production. ATP is the immediate chemical energy source used in muscle contraction; mitochondria help regenerate it aerobically, while capillaries carry blood close to the fibres. Such cellular changes help explain increased endurance capacity, but cannot be inferred directly from a pool time.

Movement learning also changes task performance. A swimmer may cover a distance more easily because coordination changed, physiological capacity changed, recent fatigue decreased, or several factors combined. The Model separates these candidate explanations rather than treating all improvement as conditioning.

## Worked example

**Constructed case:** Over several recorded occasions, a swimmer reports lower effort for the same familiar distance and time. This is consistent with improved task tolerance. It does not identify increased mitochondrial capacity: movement execution, rest and measurement conventions must also be considered. If the next occasion feels harder after less recovery, that single result does not demonstrate that all adaptation was lost. These are competing explanations of records, not instructions to perform a training experiment.

## Assumptions and limits

The account concerns ordinary learning within established swimming capabilities. No safe weekly increase, recovery duration, workload ratio, injury threshold or guaranteed timetable is derived. Persistent pain or unusual symptoms require suitable help, not a diagnosis of insufficient fitness. Dependable air access, supervision and stopping remain prerequisites; training volume cannot compensate for missing competence. A complete cardiovascular, metabolic and tissue-specific account and an individual programme remain outside scope.
