# Fewer strokes do not identify lower energy cost

## Key takeaway

Stroke count alone does not determine speed, metabolic cost or whole-stroke effectiveness.

## Summary

For the same distance, count can decrease while time increases, or while push-off distance changes. Different counts can therefore coexist with different speeds and unmeasured energy costs. Use it to frame technique questions; it does not prescribe one exact movement or establish an individual diagnosis.

## Worked use and boundary

A stronger push-off or a longer pause may reduce counted strokes without making the swim easier. Compare the same distance and start conditions, time, breathing and effort; none measures metabolic energy directly.

## Evidence and source synthesis
The non-identifiability follows from the quantities involved; it is not an estimated treatment effect. [Comparison of the arm-stroke kinematics between maximal and sub-maximal breaststroke swimming using discrete data and time series analysis](../references/gourgoulis-2022-breaststroke-arm-kinematics.md#pace-related-phase-differences) supplies a context in which pace and phase timing differ. [Freestyle Pull: The Complete Guide](../references/us-masters-swimming-undated-freestyle-pull.md) contains stronger efficiency wording that this package does not adopt.

## Derivation and applications
Record time, count convention, effort and start conditions together. Treat a lower count as a signal to interpret, not the only goal.

## Scope
Comparing controlled surface swims over known distances.

## Limits and alternatives
These simple metrics still do not measure oxygen consumption or active drag. A longer stroke can be useful, but not by definition.

## Qualification and challenge
A calibrated energetic assessment can answer a question that counts alone cannot; it does not make count alone sufficient.
