# Why floating does not guarantee horizontal balance

## Key takeaway

Water can support a body while the body turns. The total force and the turning effect of those forces answer different questions.

## Summary

This theory lesson develops one capability: explain how buoyant support can coexist with a tendency for the legs to drop. It introduces pressure, displaced water, force balance and torque, works through two contrasting cases, and finishes with a short question set. It applies the [body support and balance Model](../models/body-support-and-balance-model.md); it is not another subsystem Model or a record of anyone's learning.

Read this on land. No floating trial, breath manipulation or pool exercise is needed. Understanding the explanation does not establish swimming or recovery competence. In-water learning remains subject to [Safety](../SAFETY.md) and appropriate in-person instruction.

## The question we are trying to explain

Imagine a swimmer whose chest stays near the surface while the legs settle lower. “The legs are sinking” describes part of the movement, but it does not tell us whether the whole body lacks support. A body can change its angle while its centre remains at approximately the same height.

To understand that possibility, we need two separate questions:

- What is the combined upward and downward force on the whole body?
- Where do those forces act, and do their turning effects cancel?

We will first use a simplified body with no wall contact or active stroke. Real swimming adds movement, changing shape and other water forces. The simplification isolates the distinction; it does not predict a particular swimmer's posture.

## How water supplies support

Water pressure pushes on an immersed surface. Pressure increases with depth, so its effects around the body do not cancel vertically: their resultant is upward. That resultant is **buoyancy**. Its magnitude equals the weight of the displaced water.

“Displaced volume” means the volume occupied below the water surface, not necessarily the body's entire volume. In ordinary water with approximately uniform density:

`buoyant force = water density × gravitational acceleration × displaced volume`

**Weight** is the downward gravitational force on the body. For a motionless, freely floating body with no additional support, buoyancy balances weight. If the body moves higher, less of its volume may remain submerged, changing buoyancy until a new balance is reached.

This explains a limit of the phrase “more air makes you more buoyant.” In the simplified model, expansion that increases submerged volume increases buoyancy. After settling higher at the surface, a freely floating body can again have buoyancy equal to weight. The final support need not remain greater than weight. This is a physical distinction, not an instruction to hold or alter your breath.

**A depth comparison:** Consider the same body at the surface, with the same submerged shape and water density, above two different pool depths. Its displaced-water weight is the same. The distance to the pool floor does not enter the buoyancy expression. Access to footing and safe recovery is a separate issue; this comparison is not permission to enter deep water.

## Why equal forces can still turn a body

Replace distributed forces with two resultants:

- Weight acts through the **centre of mass**, the body's mass-weighted average position.
- Buoyancy acts through the **centre of buoyancy**, the centre of its displaced volume in uniform-density water.

These centres need not coincide. More precisely, it is the separation between the forces' **lines of action** that matters here. A line of action extends in the force's direction through its application point. Two different centres can still lie on the same vertical line.

**Torque**, also called moment, is a force's turning effect about a chosen point or axis. For a force perpendicular to its lever arm, its magnitude is force multiplied by that arm. With vertical weight and buoyancy, the relevant separation is horizontal.

## A worked contrast

The following diagrams are original schematic force diagrams, not measured swimmer anatomy. Equal arrow magnitudes are specified by the labels; the drawings are not to scale. Imagine the chest toward the left and the legs toward the right in Case B.

```text
Case A: same vertical line       Case B: separated vertical lines

        ↑ B = 600 N                     ↑ B = 600 N
        │                               │
        ● CB                            ● CB ── d ── ● CM
        │                                            │
        ● CM                                         ↓ W = 600 N
        │
        ↓ W = 600 N

CB = centre of buoyancy          CM = centre of mass
B = buoyant force                W = weight
d = horizontal distance between the vertical force lines
```

**Case A:** Upward 600 newtons and downward 600 newtons sum to zero. Their common line of action also leaves no net turning effect from this pair. For a body initially at rest, these forces alone do not start vertical or rotational acceleration. This does not establish stability: we have not examined how forces change after a disturbance.

**Case B:** The same forces still sum to zero. Now suppose their lines are 0.10 metres apart. Take moments about CM: weight has zero lever arm there, while buoyancy acts 0.10 metres to its left. The resulting torque magnitude is:

`600 N × 0.10 m = 60 N m`

In this side view, the upward force on the left and downward force on the right produce a clockwise turning tendency: the right, leg-side end tends downward. Equal force magnitudes did not remove that tendency. If the separation were 0.05 metres with the forces unchanged, the torque would be 30 N m. If it were zero, this pair would have no net torque.

The numerical values are invented for explanation. They do not estimate any person's buoyancy centres or prescribe a posture. As an actual body rotates, the submerged shape and forces can change; a constant 60 N m is not a prediction for the whole movement.

## Connecting the picture to swimming

A swimmer can therefore have enough support to remain near the surface while adopting a nonhorizontal position. Body geometry, mass distribution and submerged shape matter. Seeing low legs alone does not identify weak muscles, a faulty kick or one particular cause.

Muscles can change body configuration, which changes how the body interacts with water. They do not supply a separate external upward force merely by being tense. A hand on a wall does introduce an additional external force and possibly a moment, so supported alignment cannot simply be transferred to unsupported swimming. Active limb motion adds further fluid forces; that requires a more detailed account.

Also distinguish **acceleration** from **motion**. Zero net force means zero centre-of-mass acceleration at that instant; an already moving body does not instantly stop. Likewise, the two-force diagrams cannot tell us everything about a swimmer who is already rotating, moving limbs or passing through disturbed water.

The useful relationship is now complete: displaced water explains hydrostatic support; force balance constrains centre-of-mass acceleration; moment balance concerns turning. None alone determines a comfortable or safe stroke.

## Questions to answer together

Use the explanation above. A few sentences or a labeled sketch are enough; this is an open-note conceptual activity.

1. In Case B, why can the leg-side end begin to drop even though the two force magnitudes are equal?
2. Keep each force at 600 N but reduce their horizontal separation from 0.10 m to 0.05 m. What happens to the turning effect, and why?
3. Someone observes low legs and concludes, “Your trunk muscles must be weak.” What does the observation establish, and why does that conclusion go beyond it?

These questions examine explanation, prediction and the limit of an inference. They do not assess water confidence, swimming execution or long-term retention. Stop here to attempt the questions before reading the feedback guidance below. In live coaching, the coach should present the lesson and questions, then wait for actual replies before giving that guidance as feedback.

## Interpreting responses and choosing the next lesson

For question 1, a sufficient account distinguishes zero net force from nonzero torque caused by separated lines of action. For question 2, the magnitude halves to 30 N m under the stated fixed-force assumption; calculating alone is less informative than noticing that the lever arm halved. For question 3, low legs establish an observed position or movement, not muscle strength or a unique cause.

If an answer treats force balance as a guarantee of horizontal posture, return to the two diagrams and the lever arm. If the reasoning is correct but the arithmetic is wrong, clarify that calculation without restarting the lesson. If an inference exceeds the evidence, separate the observation from possible explanations. A confused response can also reveal unclear wording; do not automatically attribute it to the learner.

When these distinctions are usable, a connected next lesson is how posture changes the submerged shape and balance, including why a static diagram does not determine stability or moving-stroke behaviour. If the broader stage instead calls for hydrodynamic forces next, make that connection explicit. Detailed muscle physiology need not interrupt this chain of explanation. No stage completion or progression is asserted without actual learner evidence.
