# Self coach my breaststroke

## Key takeaway

Choose one safe question, use the relevant stage without link-chasing, and distinguish a reference movement from what you have actually observed.

## Summary

**Goal:** Make my breaststroke more comfortable, repeatable and understandable, while retaining safe air access and recovery. “Better” means meeting that real task—not merely more force, fewer strokes or a longer distance.

**How to use this file:** Read the safety boundary and choose the stage matching today’s question. Its card gives the goal, reason, action, observation, success signal and next step; the procedure underneath supplies the actual setup and movement. The deeper links are optional for execution, but available for explanation and evidence.

**Stage order:** Numbers show recommended reading and learning order. If prerequisites are secure, enter the stage matching the current question and revisit stages as needed. Do not attempt every stage in one session. Numbers are not stable identities.

**My current focus:** See the [Current Practice Summary](#current-practice-summary), the single place for actual experience and next decisions. Reference movements are candidates to try, not claims about my present technique.

## Safety and entry boundary
Self-coaching means I choose the question; it does **not** mean I swim alone. Use a suitable lifeguarded pool with a companion. A weak or inexperienced swimmer needs appropriate close in-person assistance; a general lifeguard presence does not replace that assistance. A kickboard is a training aid, not protection against drowning.

For face-in or supported tasks, choose an allowed area with dependable footing, a secure handhold and a known way to clear the mouth immediately. If I cannot already stop and regain air/support, I need an instructor to establish that skill before attempting an unsupported drill. Reading the stage is not evidence that I can execute it safely. I can rehearse the movement on land meanwhile.

Keep ordinary breathing and rest. Do not hyperventilate, suppress breaths, chase underwater distance or force a longer glide. Stop for pain, dizziness, unusual breathlessness, panic, disorientation, loss of support or repeated water inhalation. Regain support and get help; do not repeat a harmful condition as a control. Severe breathing difficulty or sudden severe symptoms require urgent help. Persistent or worsening pain, swelling or difficulty with ordinary movement needs appropriate healthcare assessment, not a guessed stroke correction.

No diving, racing pullouts, flip turns, open-water transfer or rehabilitation is taught here. Film only with pool permission and consent; an observer who is filming must not replace someone providing essential close safety assistance.

**Basis:** [Swimming Safety](../references/american-red-cross-undated-swimming-safety.md); [Fatal and Nonfatal Drowning Outcomes Related to Dangerous Underwater Breath-Holding Behaviors — New York State, 1988–2011](../references/boyd-2015-dangerous-underwater-breath-holding.md); [Knee pain](../references/nhs-undated-knee-pain.md); [Shoulder pain](../references/nhs-undated-shoulder-pain.md). These sources set safety boundaries, not personalized clearance.

## One comparison I can run without another document
This is an authored self-coaching procedure, not a controlled clinical or biomechanics experiment.

1. **Name one question and a finish point.** Example: whether the hands finish near the upper chest rather than continuing to the waist. Choose a few controlled cycles or a familiar short segment that I can already finish safely; there is no required length or repetition quota.
2. **Describe before explaining.** Separate “my hips felt lower” from “a side-view clip shows the hips moving down during the breath.” Neither identifies one cause. Keep a competing explanation, such as later breathing or more effort.
3. **Predict one change and one failure signal.** Write what I expect to observe, plus what would count against keeping it. Choose one cue only. A cue is a way to execute, not proof of a mechanism.
4. **Compare two safe versions.** Keep distance, start, ordinary rest and intended effort reasonably similar. Change the selected feature only as far as practical. Do not exaggerate a bad movement, create pain or restrict air. If the usual version is unsafe, do not reproduce it; use supported rehearsal or seek help instead.
5. **Check execution before outcome.** Did the intended movement actually change? If not, the hypothesis was not tested. An appropriate observer or permitted video can help, but a camera angle does not measure force or diagnose injury.
6. **Return to the whole stroke.** If a drill was used, retest at a manageable whole-stroke demand. Keep air access and the ability to stop. Rest normally; stop the session when control or attention deteriorates.
7. **Decide provisionally.** Keep, modify, discard, leave unresolved or request help. Fatigue, practice, expectation, push-off and order can explain an apparent difference. Check a promising change again on another occasion rather than declaring it proved.

No improvement is also useful information. A trial can remain inconclusive. I do not need a spreadsheet or a full research project to record one honest observation.

## Stage1 Breathing and access to air

**Summary**

**Goal:** Exhale comfortably in water and return to air without rushing.

**Why:** The above-water interval is available for inhalation when some exhalation has already happened underwater; the task must still supply enough air.

**What to do:** Use a secure handhold and stable standing; briefly immerse only as comfortable, exhale gently, clear the mouth and inhale normally.

**What to observe:** Can I exhale without force, clear the mouth deliberately and resume ordinary breathing?

**Success signal:** The supported transition is repeatable without panic, breath restriction or persistent coughing.

**Next:** Support floating and recovery for support and recovery; later Reconnect kicking with breathing or Coordinate the arms with the breath for a breath within movement.

**Entry condition**

Stable footing, an appropriate handhold and close assistance if inexperienced. If face immersion itself is frightening or uncontrolled, arrange an in-person lesson instead of repeated attempts.

**Understanding**

Breathing involves airflow and gas exchange, not merely getting the lips above water. The mouth must be clear before inhalation. For this low-demand exploration, use a comfortable mouth, nose or combined exhalation; there is no required one-second inhale, three-second exhale or forced final emptying. This selected easy starting pattern is not the only pattern used in swimming.

**Procedure**

1. Stand facing a permitted pool edge/rail with both hands secure. Settle into normal breathing with the mouth above water.
2. Take an ordinary breath, not a series of large preparatory breaths. Bend at the knees just enough to place the mouth, and later the face if comfortable, in the water while maintaining the handhold.
3. Let air leave gently through the mouth, nose or both. End the immersion before urgency; do not keep blowing until empty merely to meet a timer.
4. Straighten back to stable standing, clear the mouth and take an ordinary inhalation. Pause above water until breathing is comfortable before choosing whether to repeat.
5. Notice one issue: holding the air, blowing too hard, lifting too late or confusing mouth clearance with adequate ventilation. Do not change them all together.

**Decision points and fallback**

| If I observe… | My next decision |
|---|---|
| I cannot comfortably immerse or recover | Keep the face above water and seek qualified close instruction. Do not add a stroke. |
| Mouth clears but breathing stays unusually difficult | Stop the water test. Do not explain persistent symptoms away as timing. |

**Optional cue:** “Easy bubbles; mouth clear; breathe.” Not a timed formula.

## Stage2 Support floating and recovery

**Summary**

**Goal:** Find a comfortable supported body line and a deliberate return to standing or the wall.

**Why:** Being supported by water is not the same as lying perfectly horizontal. Recovery and air access matter more than reproducing a rigid shape.

**What to do:** Rehearse a short supported lean or float only within an already safe recovery; return the feet underneath before extending the task.

**What to observe:** Which parts remain supported, what the legs actually do, and whether I recover without a scramble.

**Success signal:** I can enter and leave the supported position calmly; I do not need to hold my breath or force the spine.

**Next:** Kick preparation and completion for the kick, or Arm preparation inward action and recovery if arm action is today’s question.

**Entry condition**

Breathing and access to air is manageable. Use a secure handhold, known standing depth and suitable close support. First-time unsupported floating/recovery needs direct instruction.

**Understanding**

Buoyancy supplies upward force; balance depends on where forces act. Legs settling below the trunk do not alone prove a fault. A comfortable long position is a reference, not a locked-core command. RNLI emergency back-floating advice concerns breathing and survival; it is not an instruction to force horizontal prone swimming or hold the breath.

**Procedure**

1. Keep a handhold and allow the torso to lean toward a face-in position only as far as the known recovery permits. A helper should support a first floating attempt, not merely watch from far away.
2. Lengthen comfortably through the arms without squeezing the ears, depressing the shoulders or arching the back. Do not demand an identical head angle or waterline.
3. While retaining support, bring the knees/feet beneath the body, place the feet on the floor and return to an upright breath. If this is unfamiliar or unreliable, stop and have it taught in person.
4. If I already control a short push-off and recovery, optionally use a gentle near-surface departure toward a predetermined safe stopping point. Do not turn it into a long underwater glide.
5. Check the return-to-length inside a few ordinary breaststroke cycles later. A pleasant supported float does not measure active drag.

**Decision points and fallback**

| If I observe… | My next decision |
|---|---|
| Legs settle while air and recovery remain controlled | Describe the position; do not add rigid bracing solely to level the legs. |
| Support or breath is lost | Regain support immediately; return to assisted skill development, not a harder glide. |

**Optional cue:** “Comfortably long; easy return.” Not “stiff as a board.”

**Basis and deeper knowledge**

Explanation: [Support versus orientation](../principles/buoyancy-and-orientation-are-distinct.md).

Model: [Support and balance](../models/body-support-and-balance-model.md).

## Stage3 Kick preparation and completion

**Summary**

**Goal:** Produce a recognizable, comfortable leg action before increasing effort.

**Why:** Foot/leg orientation changes the interaction with water. A harder effort cannot be assumed to fix incomplete preparation.

**What to do:** Recover the heels, prepare the feet comfortably, send the feet backward through the kick and finish with the legs long.

**What to observe:** Heel recovery, whether foot preparation occurs, the direction of foot travel, completion and joint comfort.

**Success signal:** The intended action is visible/repeatable and remains usable when the stroke is rejoined.

**Next:** Reconnect kicking with breathing to reconnect the kick with breathing, then Join the whole stroke for whole-stroke timing.

**Entry condition**

No painful hip/knee/ankle motion; a supported task with dependable air and recovery. Do not force turnout or copy a diagram using the hands.

**Understanding**

Coaching sources agree that foot preparation matters but describe kick trajectory differently: Swim England emphasizes a rounded action, while USMS emphasizes backward travel and individual variation in width. This Practice selects a comfortable backward-oriented kick with inward completion, not a compulsory geometric path. Propulsion sensation does not reveal a single working muscle or diagnose a knee problem.

**Procedure**

1. On land, rehearse the idea without force: heels recover, toes draw gently toward the shins and the feet turn outward within a comfortable range. Do not twist the knees or insist on a W shape.
2. For water exploration, keep a secure permitted wall hold with close support if needed. If I cannot manage the face-in position and return, stay with land rehearsal and get help.
3. Recover the heels toward the seat rather than deliberately hauling the knees to the chest. Allow the width and hip motion that are comfortable; shoulder-width knees are not a pass/fail rule.
4. Let the feet prepare before adding moderate effort. If this event is visibly missing, slow the preparation enough to notice it; do not prescribe a one-second pause.
5. Move through the kick with a backward component, then bring the legs toward a long finish. Do not interpret “squeeze” as a demand for violent knee closure or maximal power.
6. At a wall, judge movement sequence—not travel or thrust. Only later, in a safe short swim, check whether preparation and completion coexist with forward progress and normal breathing.

**Decision points and fallback**

| If I observe… | My next decision |
|---|---|
| Prepared feet but little progress | Consider timing, overall demand and observation angle. Do not assume the feet need more force or a narrower stance. |
| Knee/ankle/hip pain | Stop that movement and obtain appropriate assessment. Pain is not a successful test of a corrective cue. |

**Optional cue:** “Recover—prepare—send back—lengthen.” A reminder, not a rule about seconds or muscle activation.

**Basis and deeper knowledge**

Explanation: [Limb–water interaction](../principles/limb-configuration-changes-the-effect-of-effort.md); [Recovery cost and purpose](../principles/recovery-repositions-limbs-and-alters-resistance.md).

## Stage4 Reconnect kicking with breathing

**Summary**

**Goal:** Keep air access manageable when leg action occupies attention.

**Why:** An isolated kick removes arm and breath demands. Adding them back changes the task, so success against a wall is not yet swimming success.

**What to do:** With support, try a familiar kick, stop/recover and breathe; in an already safe stroke, retain the kick cue for a brief integrated attempt.

**What to observe:** Whether the kick stops, rushes or loses preparation when a breath is due.

**Success signal:** I can finish the selected task and breathe without relying on kick momentum to lift my mouth.

**Next:** Arm preparation inward action and recovery/B6 for arm-supported air access, or Join the whole stroke when all components are familiar.

**Entry condition**

Breathing and access to air–Kick preparation and completion are manageable in the chosen supported form. Use a board only if I can already control it and recover independently; it is not rescue support.

**Understanding**

This is a bridge exercise, not a new breaststroke rhythm. A held wall changes propulsion and balance; a board changes the body support and often the breath strategy. Neither guarantees that the mouth will rise automatically after a kick. The relevant question is whether breathing attention interrupts the prepared kick, not whether a board drill looks like the final stroke.

**Procedure**

1. At a secure wall hold, perform one comfortable kick while exhaling as appropriate. Stop the leg action, regain the known supported standing position and inhale with the mouth clear.
2. After ordinary rest, repeat only if safe. Notice whether anticipating the breath makes the feet skip preparation. Keep the trial short enough not to need a desperate head lift.
3. An optional board version is for someone already comfortable with that aid and its breathing/recovery technique. Use the familiar safe air-access method, not an invented head-up posture. Omit the board if it strains the neck or shoulders.
4. If a short whole stroke is already safe, swim it normally with one kick cue and normal breathing. If not, use Arm preparation inward action and recovery/B6 with appropriate help rather than taking the drill beyond current competence.
5. Compare what changed when breathing was added. If the movement deteriorates, reduce the task or simplify the cue instead of prolonging submersion.

**Decision points and fallback**

| If I observe… | My next decision |
|---|---|
| The kick is fine until inhalation is due | Shorten the attempt; return to a supported breath reset, then investigate Coordinate the arms with the breath. |
| The board makes the neck or breath worse | Remove that drill; use a safe alternative or ask for direct help. Equipment is not compulsory. |

**Optional cue:** “Finish safely; reset the breath.” Not “the kick will lift my head.”

**Basis and deeper knowledge**

## Stage5 Arm preparation inward action and recovery

**Summary**

**Goal:** Understand and execute a compact surface arm cycle with a clear forward return.

**Why:** The arms need a useful water-facing configuration and a route back to the front. A pull continuing far behind the upper body changes recovery demands.

**What to do:** From comfortable extension: prepare out/around, bend the elbows into the inward action, bring the hands together in front and recover forward.

**What to observe:** Where the sweep widens, where the hands finish, whether elbows and hands reorganize together, and whether recovery stalls.

**Success signal:** A recognizable cycle is comfortable and remains available when breathing is included.

**Next:** Coordinate the arms with the breath to coordinate air access, then Join the whole stroke. Use troubleshooting if the observed path is unclear.

**Entry condition**

Comfortable shoulder/elbow range; land or standing rehearsal is sufficient initially. Do not attempt arms-only floating if stopping and breathing are not already secure.

**Understanding**

The reference combines coaching descriptions of preparation, inward action and recovery. A hand may pitch or travel downward while the forearm reorganizes; that is not identical to pressing the entire body abruptly upward. “Never move down” and “keep the elbows fixed” are therefore poor literal constraints. A compact action means avoiding an unnecessarily large excursion, not pinning the elbows to the ribs.

**Procedure**

1. Rehearse on land or standing in permitted water with stable footing. Reach comfortably forward; keep hands close without forcing an overhead shoulder position.
2. Separate and pitch the hands outward, allowing the forearms to orient as the elbows bend. Think of preparing a surface to interact with the water, not making the widest possible circle.
3. Bring the hands and forearms inward toward the front of the upper chest while the elbows move inward as needed. Do not drag the hands along to the waist and then pull them back up.
4. As the hands come close, guide them directly ahead toward comfortable extension. For this reference, recovery can remain near/under the surface; no throwing the hands upward is needed.
5. Choose one event to check: excessive width, a far-back finish or a delayed forward return. Land rehearsal cannot show the actual water forces.
6. If safe, use a few ordinary breaststroke cycles with the same one cue, rather than a long arms-only effort. Observe whether the actual stroke retained the reference.

**Decision points and fallback**

| If I observe… | My next decision |
|---|---|
| Smaller pull produces less usable air access | Do not shrink it further. Investigate the breath/body timing in Coordinate the arms with the breath. |
| I feel a big downward shove or pitching motion | Confirm the event with suitable observation; reduce effort and check preparation, head motion and timing before choosing a cause. |

**Optional cue:** “Prepare—gather in front—reach ahead.” The cue is optional; the observed movement matters.

## Stage6 Coordinate the arms with the breath

**Summary**

**Goal:** Get a comfortable breath as the arm/body action rises and returns, without an emergency neck lift.

**Why:** Air access and arm recovery share a short part of the cycle. Delaying the breath can change the next movement even if the isolated arms look sound.

**What to do:** Exhale comfortably while immersed, inhale only when the mouth clears during the inward/body-rise phase, and return the face as the hands go forward.

**What to observe:** Whether the breath begins late, the head remains back after recovery starts, or the hands wait for inhalation.

**Success signal:** The mouth clears, inhalation finishes without rushing and forward recovery continues comfortably.

**Next:** Join the whole stroke for the full sequence; return to Breathing and access to air if the supported breath itself is unreliable.

**Entry condition**

Breathing and access to air and the chosen arm rehearsal are manageable. A standing arm/breath rehearsal can precede swimming, but its head movement does not prove a floating technique.

**Understanding**

The reference coordinates head/neck position with torso and shoulder movement; it does not ask the neck to lever the body out of the water. The head is not motionless, and buoyancy does not guarantee an effortless mouth lift. Breathing frequency, pace and timing remain relevant. A gentle underwater exhalation is the selected starting option, not a mandated nasal-only or explosive pattern.

**Procedure**

1. In standing supported rehearsal, trace the arm cycle and notice where an inhale would occur; keep the neck comfortable. Do not press down harder to manufacture lift.
2. During a safe short whole-stroke attempt, allow the arm/body rise to bring the mouth clear. Inhale through the mouth only after clearance; abandon the attempt rather than inhale through water.
3. As the hands recover ahead, let the face return in coordination and resume comfortable exhalation. Do not force the chin onto the chest or lock the shoulders down.
4. Ask an allowed observer, or inspect permitted side video later, for event order: mouth clear, inhalation, hands forward, face return. A side view cannot fully describe a three-dimensional hand path.
5. If the head stays raised after the hands reach forward, test an earlier, unhurried breath at lower demand. Change timing rather than adding a second body-position cue.
6. Check that a smoother-looking return also preserves air and kick timing. If not, the change is not ready to keep.

**Decision points and fallback**

| If I observe… | My next decision |
|---|---|
| Mouth fails to clear | Restore support. Check actual clearance, effort and arm/body timing; do not prescribe stronger downward pressure. |
| Mouth clears but I gasp or cough repeatedly | Stop. Return to supported air access if well, or seek appropriate help for persistent/unusual symptoms. |

**Optional cue:** “Air with the rise; face back with the reach.” Not a force or anatomy explanation.

**Basis and deeper knowledge**

## Stage7 Join the whole stroke

**Summary**

**Goal:** Connect the leg, arm and breath events without turning a memory cue into four separate pauses.

**Why:** Timing changes how each action interacts with the rest. A successful component is not sufficient evidence of a workable cycle.

**What to do:** Pull and breathe; recover the hands as the legs prepare; send the kick into the returning long body line; finish before starting the next cycle.

**What to observe:** The first event that breaks down: breath, forward recovery, foot preparation, kick completion or the next pull.

**Success signal:** Several manageable cycles retain the chosen movement, ordinary breathing and a deliberate safe finish.

**Next:** Continuity repeatability and distance for continuity/demand, or revisit the specific component rather than restarting every stage.

**Entry condition**

An appropriately supported or independently safe short stroke is available. If combining actions removes safe recovery, seek instruction rather than rehearsing failure.

**Understanding**

“Pull–breathe–kick–glide” is an attention cue. Some recovery movements overlap, so “arms move while the legs remain perfectly still” is not a universal law. A useful easy reference is for the hands to be travelling toward extension as the feet prepare, with the effective kick meeting a returning body line. The exact overlap varies with swimmer and task; no video phase map determines one beginner timing.

**Procedure**

1. Choose a familiar short route with a safe endpoint. Begin at an effort that leaves enough attention for one event relationship.
2. Let the inward arm action and body rise provide the breathing opportunity. Do not add a forceful simultaneous kick just to lift the mouth.
3. As the hands gather and return forward, allow heel recovery and foot preparation without deliberately dragging the knees to the chest.
4. As the arms approach a comfortable forward position, complete the prepared kick and lengthen the legs. Treat this as a starting coordination reference, not a stop-until-perfect-extension command.
5. Recognize the completed long position, then begin the next cycle before waiting creates an unmanageable gap. Do not force a two- or three-second glide.
6. If the sequence fails, identify the earliest repeatable disruption. Rehearse just that event relationship in an easier version, then retest the whole stroke with the same breath access.
7. Later try without the cue. A movement that requires continuous conscious counting may not yet be robust at the chosen demand.

**Decision points and fallback**

| If I observe… | My next decision |
|---|---|
| Foot preparation vanishes when arms return | Return to Kick preparation and completion/B4, then retest a shorter whole-stroke attempt. |
| I obey the phrase but the stroke stops at each word | Use one event cue, such as “hands forward as feet prepare”; do not impose four holds. |

**Optional cue:** “Pull and breathe; reach and prepare; kick to length.” This is a selected learning reference, not a competition specification.

## Stage8 Continuity repeatability and distance

**Summary**

**Goal:** Choose a manageable rhythm and extend it only while the important controls survive.

**Why:** Fewer strokes can simply mean more waiting or a different push-off; neither count nor completed distance establishes lower energy cost.

**What to do:** Compare one small timing or demand change at a familiar short task, then check time, count convention, effort and breathing together.

**What to observe:** Whether kick completion is cut short, the next pull is delayed, or technique changes as fatigue rises.

**Success signal:** The selected rhythm remains controlled and useful in ordinary swimming on more than one occasion.

**Next:** Keep provisionally, return to the first limiting stage or request a specific outside observation.

**Entry condition**

Join the whole stroke is controlled at the current distance. A familiar stop and normal breathing remain available; no long underwater glide or distance quota.

**Understanding**

Continuity is a trade-off, not a seconds target. Waiting may reduce count while allowing speed to decay; hurrying may interrupt preparation or completion. Small personal comparisons can support a choice without proving reduced drag or energy cost. Research on trained swimmers does not supply a universal beginner optimum.

**Procedure**

1. Use the same familiar start, gentle push-off and short surface segment. Define a breaststroke count as one full arm-and-leg cycle and keep that definition unchanged.
2. Describe the current timing: do the hands start a new pull before the legs finish, or is there a prolonged dead interval? Confirm the event rather than infer it from effort alone.
3. Compare one modest, comfortable adjustment—allowing completion if rushed, or beginning the next cycle a little sooner if clearly waiting. Neither test should create urgency for air.
4. Record time only if useful, plus count, effort, breath comfort and whether the intended timing changed. Rest normally and acknowledge different starts/order/fatigue.
5. For greater distance, change only one demand: a small extension or another comfortable repeat. Use only a wall transition I already know safely; there is no need to add a racing turn.
6. If form or breathing deteriorates, finish safely and return to the manageable task. Note the first failure event and route back to its stage.
7. Recheck a promising choice later without forced counting. Record it as a provisional personal choice, not a scientific discovery.

**Decision points and fallback**

| If I observe… | My next decision |
|---|---|
| Count falls but effort rises or time worsens | Do not label the change more efficient. Reconsider the goal and timing. |
| The last part becomes a survival finish | The task is too demanding now. Shorten it and restore ordinary rest/air access. |

**Optional cue:** “Complete; continue; stay in control.” Not “glide as long as possible.”

**Basis and deeper knowledge**

Explanation: [Count versus energy](../principles/fewer-strokes-do-not-identify-lower-energy-cost.md); [Timing interactions](../principles/relative-timing-changes-the-whole-stroke.md).

Model: [Measurements](../models/stroke-measurement-model.md); [Self-coaching loop](../models/self-coaching-model.md).

### When the cause remains uncertain
Name two plausible explanations or explicitly write “not enough observation.” Seek one useful external view, simplify the demand or stop investigating for today. Do not invent a physical mechanism from a sensation, a single frame or a cue that happened to feel good. A source may inform the hypothesis without confirming my current movement.

## Local evidence review

The movement descriptions are externally informed references; the staged self-coaching sequence is authored synthesis. Swim England and U.S. Masters Swimming provide coaching guidance, not comparative trials of this full Practice. Their descriptions differ in places: a rounded versus more directly backward breaststroke kick, and thumb-first versus relatively flat front-crawl entry. No exact gaze angle, knee width, hand path, breath count or glide duration is made universally mandatory here.

The head-position study used passive towing in experienced male swimmers. The arm-phase study available at abstract level compared intensities in nine male breaststrokers. Neither establishes an individual beginner prescription. Breath-restriction and no-breath drill variants from racing-oriented guidance are excluded. A visually improved movement, a lower stroke count or easier sensation is not by itself proof of a mechanism or lower metabolic cost.
