# Self coach my freestyle front crawl

## Key takeaway

Choose one safe question, use the relevant stage without link-chasing, and distinguish a reference movement from what you have actually observed.

## Summary

**Goal:** Make my freestyle (front crawl) more comfortable, repeatable and understandable, while retaining safe air access and recovery. “Better” means meeting that real task—not merely more force, fewer strokes or a longer distance.

**How to use this file:** Read the safety boundary and choose the stage matching today’s question. Its card gives the goal, reason, action, observation, success signal and next step; the procedure underneath supplies the actual setup and movement. The deeper links are optional for execution, but available for explanation and evidence.

**Stage order:** Numbers show recommended reading and learning order. If prerequisites are secure, enter the stage matching the current question and revisit stages as needed. Do not attempt every stage in one session. Numbers are not stable identities.

**My current focus:** See the [Current Practice Summary](#current-practice-summary), the single place for actual experience and next decisions. Reference movements are candidates to try, not claims about my present technique.

## Safety and entry boundary
Self-coaching means I choose the question; it does **not** mean I swim alone. Use a suitable lifeguarded pool with a companion. A weak or inexperienced swimmer needs appropriate close in-person assistance; a general lifeguard presence does not replace that assistance. A kickboard is a training aid, not protection against drowning.

For face-in or supported tasks, choose an allowed area with dependable footing, a secure handhold and a known way to clear the mouth immediately. If I cannot already stop and regain air/support, I need an instructor to establish that skill before attempting an unsupported drill. Reading the stage is not evidence that I can execute it safely. I can rehearse the movement on land meanwhile.

Keep ordinary breathing and rest. Do not hyperventilate, suppress breaths, chase underwater distance or force a longer glide. Stop for pain, dizziness, unusual breathlessness, panic, disorientation, loss of support or repeated water inhalation. Regain support and get help; do not repeat a harmful condition as a control. Severe breathing difficulty or sudden severe symptoms require urgent help. Persistent or worsening pain, swelling or difficulty with ordinary movement needs appropriate healthcare assessment, not a guessed stroke correction.

No diving, racing pullouts, flip turns, open-water transfer or rehabilitation is taught here. Film only with pool permission and consent; an observer who is filming must not replace someone providing essential close safety assistance.

**Basis:** [Swimming Safety](../references/american-red-cross-undated-swimming-safety.md); [Fatal and Nonfatal Drowning Outcomes Related to Dangerous Underwater Breath-Holding Behaviors — New York State, 1988–2011](../references/boyd-2015-dangerous-underwater-breath-holding.md); [Knee pain](../references/nhs-undated-knee-pain.md); [Shoulder pain](../references/nhs-undated-shoulder-pain.md). These sources set safety boundaries, not personalized clearance.

## One comparison I can run without another document
This is an authored self-coaching procedure, not a controlled clinical or biomechanics experiment.

1. **Name one question and a finish point.** Example: whether the entering hand stays on its own side of the head instead of crossing the center line. Choose a few controlled cycles or a familiar short segment that I can already finish safely; there is no required length or repetition quota.
2. **Describe before explaining.** Separate “my hips felt lower” from “a side-view clip shows the hips moving down during the breath.” Neither identifies one cause. Keep a competing explanation, such as later breathing or more effort.
3. **Predict one change and one failure signal.** Write what I expect to observe, plus what would count against keeping it. Choose one cue only. A cue is a way to execute, not proof of a mechanism.
4. **Compare two safe versions.** Keep distance, start, ordinary rest and intended effort reasonably similar. Change the selected feature only as far as practical. Do not exaggerate a bad movement, create pain or restrict air. If the usual version is unsafe, do not reproduce it; use supported rehearsal or seek help instead.
5. **Check execution before outcome.** Did the intended movement actually change? If not, the hypothesis was not tested. An appropriate observer or permitted video can help, but a camera angle does not measure force or diagnose injury.
6. **Return to the whole stroke.** If a drill was used, retest at a manageable whole-stroke demand. Keep air access and the ability to stop. Rest normally; stop the session when control or attention deteriorates.
7. **Decide provisionally.** Keep, modify, discard, leave unresolved or request help. Fatigue, practice, expectation, push-off and order can explain an apparent difference. Check a promising change again on another occasion rather than declaring it proved.

No improvement is also useful information. A trial can remain inconclusive. I do not need a spreadsheet or a full research project to record one honest observation.

## Stage1 Breathing with reliable support

**Summary**

**Goal:** Build a comfortable face-in exhalation and clear-mouth inhalation before adding stroke demands.

**Why:** Airflow, access to air and effort are separate contributors; a head-turn cue alone cannot solve all breathing difficulties.

**What to do:** With a handhold and stable footing, briefly immerse as comfortable, exhale gently, return to air and breathe normally.

**What to observe:** Whether I hold the air, empty forcefully, rush to inhale or cannot recover calmly.

**Success signal:** The transition is comfortable and repeatable without breath restriction or persistent coughing.

**Next:** Body support and a usable forward line for support/body line or Connect side breathing to the arm and body turn when the next question is side breathing.

**Entry condition**

A secure poolside hold, reliable standing and close in-person assistance if inexperienced. If I cannot clear the mouth immediately, do not progress by adding repetitions.

**Understanding**

The selected low-demand reference is ordinary inhalation above water and comfortable exhalation while immersed. Air can leave through nose, mouth or both. No one-second/three-second formula is necessary. The important observable is usable breathing, not a bubble quota or forced emptying of the lungs.

**Procedure**

1. Stand at an allowed rail/edge with both hands secure and mouth above water. Settle into normal breathing.
2. After an ordinary inhalation, bend enough to immerse the mouth or face only as comfortable. Retain footing/hand support and a known immediate recovery.
3. Release a gentle exhalation. Do not stay immersed until empty or breathless and do not take rapid large breaths in preparation.
4. Return upright with the mouth clear and inhale normally. Rest until settled. Choose whether another short attempt would actually answer the question.
5. Once this is comfortable, inspect whether breathing becomes difficult only when the head turns or effort rises. Those require a different stage, not harder blowing.

**Decision points and fallback**

| If I observe… | My next decision |
|---|---|
| Face immersion is frightening or recovery is uncertain | Stop independent experiments and establish this with qualified in-person help. |
| Breathlessness persists despite easy clearance | Stop and obtain appropriate assessment when unusual/persistent; do not diagnose it as a neck-angle issue. |

**Optional cue:** “Easy out; clear mouth; breathe.” Not a duration target.

## Stage2 Body support and a usable forward line

**Summary**

**Goal:** Return to a comfortable body line without creating a rigid pose or losing safe recovery.

**Why:** Floating support, orientation and active movement are different. A still photograph of level legs is not the whole swimming task.

**What to do:** Use a supported lean/float or a previously mastered short near-surface glide, then check the same position during a safe stroke.

**What to observe:** Neck/shoulder comfort, easy recovery, body motion during a breath and whether a long reach becomes forced.

**Success signal:** The position is repeatable and leaves both air access and arm movement available.

**Next:** A manageable flutter kick for the kick or Arm entry preparation underwater action and recovery for arm action; Connect side breathing to the arm and body turn if the line changes mainly during breathing.

**Entry condition**

Breathing with reliable support is manageable and recovery to standing/wall is already reliable in the chosen task. First-time floating or unsupported recovery needs direct instruction.

**Understanding**

Body line is a working configuration rather than a perfect straight line. A swimmer may float with lower legs. A passive towing result does not establish an ideal active head angle. The practical target here is a comfortable forward return that can coexist with rotation and breathing, not maximum tension or a forced chin-down lever.

**Procedure**

1. Use a secure handhold and dependable standing depth. With appropriate close support, lean or float only within the recovery already mastered.
2. Reach forward comfortably without clamping the shoulders or pushing the chest down. Keep the neck unforced; do not imitate a fixed hairline water level.
3. Return the feet beneath the body while holding support and regain upright air access. If this cannot be done calmly, stop and obtain help.
4. If already competent, a gentle short near-surface push-off can show the return position. End at a planned safe point; no underwater distance or breath-hold test.
5. In a familiar short crawl, notice whether the body line changes primarily when breathing, when kicking harder or when overreaching. Pick one event for the next stage.

**Decision points and fallback**

| If I observe… | My next decision |
|---|---|
| Legs settle in a supported float | Do not assume weak core muscles or prescribe bracing; assess safe support first. |
| A longer reach strains the shoulder | Shorten the reach and stop painful motion. A picture of alignment does not justify pain. |

**Optional cue:** “Reach comfortably; keep a way back to air.”

**Basis and deeper knowledge**

Explanation: [Support versus orientation](../principles/buoyancy-and-orientation-are-distinct.md); [Resistance and configuration](../principles/resistance-varies-with-speed-and-configuration.md).

Model: [Support and balance](../models/body-support-and-balance-model.md).

Full Method: [Check body line](../methods/check-body-line.md).

## Stage3 A manageable flutter kick

**Summary**

**Goal:** Find alternating leg action that does not consume the breathing and attention needed for the arms.

**Why:** The kick participates in motion and body control, but extra splash or effort does not guarantee a better whole stroke.

**What to do:** Explore a modest alternating kick with comfortable hip, knee and ankle movement, then reintegrate with normal breathing.

**What to observe:** Whether the movement starts mainly as knee cycling, whether the feet leave the water excessively, and when the kick becomes frantic.

**Success signal:** The kick remains manageable in the full task without forced feet, locked knees or worsening breath comfort.

**Next:** Arm entry preparation underwater action and recovery or Connect side breathing to the arm and body turn for the limiting component, then Make the whole stroke repeatable for integration.

**Entry condition**

A supported position with a known breath/recovery route, or an already safe short crawl. No vertical kicking or ankle restraints.

**Understanding**

The selected reference is small alternating movement along the body line. The hips participate and the knees bend naturally; “straight legs” should not mean locked knees. Coaching descriptions differ in amplitude and rhythm. No fixed kick count, depth or joint angle is imposed. At a held wall, movement can be observed but forward propulsion cannot be measured.

**Procedure**

1. At a secure wall hold with appropriate support, or during a familiar short crawl, begin with manageable alternating leg movement rather than maximum effort.
2. Let the legs move from the hip with comfortable knee flexion and relaxed ankles. Avoid pulling the knees toward the chest as though cycling; do not force pointed toes.
3. Keep the action near the surface with the feet interacting with the water. Noise and splash are not the target.
4. Stop and recover air/support before attention or breathing deteriorates. Compare one comfortable change in effort or excursion only if both versions are safe.
5. Return to ordinary crawl and check whether the same kick still permits the arm cycle and a usable breath. If it becomes frantic only at inhalation, investigate Connect side breathing to the arm and body turn instead of merely strengthening the kick.

**Decision points and fallback**

| If I observe… | My next decision |
|---|---|
| Supported kicking is comfortable but the full stroke is not | The drill removed demands; retest a shorter integrated task in Make the whole stroke repeatable. |
| Less splash produces worse control or more effort | The change is unresolved, not automatically more efficient. |

**Optional cue:** “Small, alternating, comfortable.” Not “kick harder until the legs rise.”

**Basis and deeper knowledge**

Explanation: [Timing interactions](../principles/relative-timing-changes-the-whole-stroke.md); [Limits of isolated success](../principles/isolated-success-does-not-establish-transfer.md).

Model: [Front-crawl cycle](../models/front-crawl-cycle-model.md).

## Stage4 Arm entry preparation underwater action and recovery

**Summary**

**Goal:** Establish an arm cycle that can repeat with body rotation and later breathing.

**Why:** Entry places the limb for the next interaction. Forcing one geometric path or reaching across the body can change the rest of the cycle.

**What to do:** Enter comfortably ahead of the same-side shoulder region, prepare the hand/forearm, direct the underwater action back and recover without strain.

**What to observe:** Entry track, whether preparation is rushed, a forced shoulder position and the return toward the next entry.

**Success signal:** The chosen action is comfortable and observable in short ordinary swimming, including the breath.

**Next:** Connect side breathing to the arm and body turn for breathing, then Make the whole stroke repeatable for whole-stroke transfer.

**Entry condition**

Comfortable shoulder/elbow motion and either stable standing rehearsal or a safe short integrated stroke. Do not remove essential support for an arms-only drill.

**Understanding**

Sources disagree about exact entry pitch and hand path. The selected starting reference is fingertips entering with a fairly neutral palm orientation near the same-side shoulder track; this is not proof of superiority. Preparation involves the forearm as well as the hand. “High elbow” is not an instruction to freeze a joint angle, twist the shoulder or force the arm against pain.

**Procedure**

1. On land or in stable standing, trace one arm forward at a time. Avoid reaching across the head midline or chasing the farthest possible extension.
2. In a safe short crawl, let the hand enter forward with a slight downward path and a comfortable elbow. Do not slap down with a flat palm or require a thumb-first twist.
3. After entry, let the hand/forearm orient for the underwater action as the elbow bends comfortably. Avoid applying maximal effort immediately at entry or only pushing vertically down.
4. Move through the underwater action toward the same-side hip region within comfortable range; do not draw a compulsory S/hourglass pattern or force an exaggerated finish behind the body.
5. Let the arm clear and travel forward in coordination with body roll. Do not pin the shoulder down or require the highest possible recovering elbow.
6. Use one cue on a few controlled cycles with normal breathing. A front-oblique view may clarify crossover; a side-only view can hide it. Stop to review rather than looking at a camera while swimming.

**Decision points and fallback**

| If I observe… | My next decision |
|---|---|
| Entry crosses only during the breath | Investigate turn timing in Connect side breathing to the arm and body turn, not just an isolated hand correction. |
| Catch or recovery is painful | Stop painful motion and seek appropriate help. More force or shoulder stretching is not the next test. |

**Optional cue:** “Enter on my own track; prepare; send water back; recover easily.”

**Basis and deeper knowledge**

Explanation: [Limb–water interaction](../principles/limb-configuration-changes-the-effect-of-effort.md); [Recovery cost and purpose](../principles/recovery-repositions-limbs-and-alters-resistance.md).

Model: [Front-crawl cycle](../models/front-crawl-cycle-model.md); [Force and resistance](../models/force-and-resistance-model.md).

Full Method: [Explore front crawl arms](../methods/explore-front-crawl-arms.md).

## Stage5 Connect side breathing to the arm and body turn

**Summary**

**Goal:** Access air on a comfortable side without a rushed lift or long pause in the stroke.

**Why:** Breathing changes head/body orientation during an ongoing arm cycle. Clear-mouth access and enough ventilation take priority over visual symmetry.

**What to do:** Exhale underwater, turn with the body as the breathing-side arm moves toward recovery, inhale when clear and return the face before the next entry.

**What to observe:** Whether the turn is late, the lead arm collapses downward, the neck over-rotates or the mouth stays out after recovery.

**Success signal:** The breath is available without panic and the alternating stroke resumes without a forced pause.

**Next:** Make the whole stroke repeatable for integration; Breathing with reliable support if exhalation or supported air access is the limiting issue.

**Entry condition**

Breathing with reliable support is manageable and the chosen short task has a safe stop. If head turning cannot be explored without losing air/support, use an instructor-assisted rehearsal.

**Understanding**

A turn accompanying body roll is the reference, not lifting straight forward or twisting only the neck. The recovery-side arm gives an event to coordinate with: do not wait until it is about to re-enter before searching for air. Returning the face before that hand enters is a useful starting checkpoint, not a universal millisecond target. A bow-wave trough may help at some speeds, but is not guaranteed for a slow learner.

**Procedure**

1. Rehearse the turn on land with comfortable shoulder/body rotation. Keep the head broadly aligned rather than craning backward. Rehearsal teaches sequence, not proof of water clearance.
2. Use a familiar, easy short crawl with normal exhalation. Select the easier breathing side first; there is no prerequisite to breathe bilaterally.
3. As the breathing-side arm progresses back and begins recovery, turn the head with the body enough to clear the mouth. Let the opposite arm continue its coordinated role rather than forcing it to press down as a support pole.
4. Inhale only when clear. Return the face smoothly as the recovering arm comes forward, aiming to have the face back before hand entry without rushing air access.
5. Use a frequency that supplies air. Breathing on successive returns of the comfortable side is an available reference; every-third-stroke breathing is not compulsory. Do not extend the interval to prove discipline.
6. Observe one breath event, then stop safely. If turning earlier helps clearance but arm rhythm or comfort worsens, the change needs refinement rather than automatic adoption.

**Decision points and fallback**

| If I observe… | My next decision |
|---|---|
| I need a large last-second lift | Shorten/lower the task demand and investigate earlier turn timing; obtain close help if clearance remains unreliable. |
| The mouth is clear but air still feels insufficient | Reconsider effort and the exhalation pattern; stop for persistent/unusual symptoms. Do not assume a particular goggle position is the cure. |

**Optional cue:** “Turn with the stroke; get air; return before the hand.” Never suppress a needed breath to satisfy the cue.

**Basis and deeper knowledge**

## Stage6 Make the whole stroke repeatable

**Summary**

**Goal:** Keep the useful component change when arms, legs, body roll and breathing work together.

**Why:** A drill may succeed because it removes the exact interaction causing difficulty. Integration checks the real task.

**What to do:** Use a short ordinary crawl, one targeted cue or easier rehearsal, then another normal-breathing whole-stroke attempt.

**What to observe:** First loss of rhythm, breath-related scissor kick, entry change and whether the intended adjustment actually appears.

**Success signal:** The change survives a manageable whole stroke and later use without constant correction.

**Next:** Refine demand measure usefully and progress for repeatability/demand, or the first stage whose event breaks down.

**Entry condition**

Enough control to complete and stop a short full stroke with normal breathing; otherwise work with appropriate in-person support.

**Understanding**

Continuous alternation does not mean fixed spacing between arms or one required kick rhythm. Some overlap and body motion are part of swimming. Avoid creating an artificial catch-up pause simply because a preparatory drill used a long lead arm. The relevant observation is the relationship that fails during the breath or fatigue, not whether the stroke matches a still photograph.

**Procedure**

1. Swim a familiar short segment at easy effort. Observe one event—such as lead-arm movement during a breath—rather than the whole technique at once.
2. Select only the stage that addresses the observed event. Use its easier rehearsal briefly if helpful; do not complete all drills as a mandatory checklist.
3. Return to the same manageable whole stroke with normal air access. Keep one cue, such as earlier turn timing, rather than combining hand entry, knee motion and head angle changes.
4. Check that the intended action happened and that breathing, direction and comfort were retained. A different push-off or harder kick may explain a faster time.
5. If safe and useful, try again later without the cue. A good repetition while concentrating intensely does not establish a durable habit.
6. Record a provisional decision or unresolved question. If a component repeatedly fails despite clear rehearsal, request one targeted external observation.

**Decision points and fallback**

| If I observe… | My next decision |
|---|---|
| The drill feels good but the breath disrupts the stroke again | Return to Connect side breathing to the arm and body turn with a shorter whole task; do not claim the drill transferred. |
| Several things break down together | Lower the task demand and inspect the first visible disruption, or seek observation rather than guessing several corrections. |

**Optional cue:** “One question; ordinary breathing; check the whole stroke.”

**Basis and deeper knowledge**

Explanation: [Limits of isolated success](../principles/isolated-success-does-not-establish-transfer.md); [Timing interactions](../principles/relative-timing-changes-the-whole-stroke.md).

Model: [Front-crawl cycle](../models/front-crawl-cycle-model.md); [Self-coaching loop](../models/self-coaching-model.md).

Full Method: [Integrate front crawl](../methods/integrate-front-crawl.md); [Check whole stroke transfer](../methods/check-whole-stroke-transfer.md).

## Stage7 Refine demand measure usefully and progress

**Summary**

**Goal:** Extend comfortable swimming without mistaking distance or low stroke count for technical proof.

**Why:** Metrics describe different things; a lower count can coexist with a slower, harder swim, and more distance can conceal loss of control.

**What to do:** Change one manageable demand, keep ordinary breathing/rest, and compare execution plus task-level signals.

**What to observe:** Where technique begins to fade, whether breathing stays available, and whether starts/count conventions are comparable.

**Success signal:** The intended technique remains usable at the chosen demand across more than one occasion.

**Next:** Keep provisionally, shorten the task or return to the relevant stage. Record the next question.

**Entry condition**

Make the whole stroke repeatable is controlled at the current task. No compulsory full length, reduced-breathing set or new racing turn.

**Understanding**

For front crawl, decide whether the count means each hand entry or a complete two-arm cycle. Time and count are not interchangeable with energy expenditure. My comparison can be useful without controlling every variable, provided I state what changed and do not call an apparent benefit a causal proof.

**Procedure**

1. Choose a familiar short segment and one explicit counting convention. Keep the start, push-off and stopping point similar; otherwise record the difference.
2. Note effort and breath comfort alongside time/count only when useful. Do not stare at a watch or manipulate recording equipment while swimming.
3. For progression, add a modest distance or another comfortable repeat, not harder effort and shorter rest at the same time. Normal breathing and a planned safe stop remain non-negotiable.
4. Use only a wall stop/turn I already control. If the wall interrupts air access, stop, reset at the wall and seek instruction rather than adding a flip turn.
5. At the first deterioration in breath, direction, pain-free motion or recovery, finish safely. Return to the prior manageable task and identify the first changed event.
6. Revisit a promising adjustment later. Update only the actual personal observation and decision; leave untested technique fields blank.

**Decision points and fallback**

| If I observe… | My next decision |
|---|---|
| Lower count but more waiting and higher effort | Do not label it improved efficiency. Compare the actual goal and whole task. |
| The last part requires gasping or scrambling | The task is beyond current control. Shorten it and recover, rather than finish the quota. |

**Optional cue:** “Finish in control, not at any cost.”

**Basis and deeper knowledge**

### When the cause remains uncertain
Name two plausible explanations or explicitly write “not enough observation.” Seek one useful external view, simplify the demand or stop investigating for today. Do not invent a physical mechanism from a sensation, a single frame or a cue that happened to feel good. A source may inform the hypothesis without confirming my current movement.

## Evidence scope and maintenance
This file is a context-specific synthesis, not a transcription of a single source. Coaching references describe candidate movement; physical/physiological Models explain possible mechanisms; research has population/task limits; the stage sequence and comparison procedures are authored design. Common wording across pages from one organization is not independent experimental evidence.

No prescribed knee width, forced joint angle, exact gaze angle, glide duration, universal breathing interval or automatic pain diagnosis follows from this Practice. Safe variation and unresolved source disagreements remain explicit. Nothing certifies ability, proves an energy saving or clears a medical condition.

## Local evidence review

The movement descriptions are externally informed references; the staged self-coaching sequence is authored synthesis. Swim England and U.S. Masters Swimming provide coaching guidance, not comparative trials of this full Practice. Their descriptions differ in places: a rounded versus more directly backward breaststroke kick, and thumb-first versus relatively flat front-crawl entry. No exact gaze angle, knee width, hand path, breath count or glide duration is made universally mandatory here.

The head-position study used passive towing in experienced male swimmers. The arm-phase study available at abstract level compared intensities in nine male breaststrokers. Neither establishes an individual beginner prescription. Breath-restriction and no-breath drill variants from racing-oriented guidance are excluded. A visually improved movement, a lower stroke count or easier sensation is not by itself proof of a mechanism or lower metabolic cost.
